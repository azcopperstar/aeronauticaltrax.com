The aircraft

What an airplane actually needs tracked.

Not a mileage counter with different labels. Aircraft maintenance runs on hours, calendar months and recurring directives at the same time, per component — so that is how AeroTrax models it.

Hangar view

Your whole fleet at a glance — tail number, registration, make and model, specs and photos.

Hobbs & tach time

Both clocks tracked and kept current automatically from flight and fuel entries, rather than typed in twice.

Airworthiness directives

One-time and recurring ADs, each with its own compliance schedule, so a recurring AD comes due like any other interval.

Inspection cycles

Annual, 100-hour, pitot-static, transponder, altimeter and ELT — every cycle on its own clock, calendar or hours.

Component times

Airframe, engine and propeller carry their own total time and time since overhaul, tracked per component.

Flight log

Route, duration, en-route stops, fuel consumed and trip grouping — the aircraft side of every flight you make.

Fuel log

Fill-ups, cost, economy over time and fluid checks, tied to the flights they belong to.

Combined logbook PDF

ADs, inspection status and component times in a single document — the one you hand over when someone asks.

The parts

Every part, and the paper that proves it.

A part is only airworthy if you can show where it came from. AeroTrax keeps the 8130-3, the approval basis and the trace back to the source on the part record itself — next to the hours, the cycles and the life limit. When someone asks you to prove it, the answer is a tap rather than an afternoon in a filing cabinet.

Approval tags on file

The FAA Form 8130-3, EASA Form 1 or Certificate of Conformity attached to the part record itself, photographed or imported — so the paperwork travels with the part instead of living in a shoebox.

Approval basis

OEM, PMA, TSO article, approved under an STC, owner-produced, or standard AN/MS/NAS hardware. Recorded as a field of its own, because it is the difference between a part you may install and one you may not.

Source traceability

The chain back to the production approval holder, vendor by vendor. The record a pre-purchase inspection asks for first, and the one that keeps a suspected unapproved part out of your airplane.

Part numbers that change

Supersession tracked, so a revised dash number links back to the part it replaces. Alternates and interchangeables, lot and batch numbers for parts that carry no serial, and an ATA chapter on everything.

Life limits kept separate from TBO

A life limit is a discard. A TBO is a manufacturer’s recommendation. An inspection interval is neither. All three are modeled separately rather than flattened into one due date that hides which is which.

Hours, cycles and calendar

Time since new, overhaul, repair and inspection, plus cycles for the parts that count landings rather than hours — each running against the clock it actually accrues on, Hobbs or tach or air time.

Derived, not typed twice

A part’s time is computed from the aircraft time recorded at installation, so it stays correct on its own. Nothing to keep in step by hand, and nothing to drift the first time an entry is missed.

Directives that follow the part

Many airworthiness directives are issued against a part number, not an airframe. Linking them to the part means a recurring AD cannot go quiet when a component moves to another aircraft.

Condition and shelf life

New, overhauled, repaired, serviceable, as-removed or scrap — with cure dates and expiry for hoses, seals, tires, batteries and oxygen. A part can be new in the box and still not airworthy.

The records 14 CFR 91.417 says must stay with the aircraft — total time in service, the current status of life-limited parts, time since overhaul, inspection status and AD status — come out of AeroTrax as one report. It is also the first thing a buyer’s mechanic asks for.

Also on every part record

  • Installed position — engine number, gear leg, cylinder, with its next higher assembly
  • Removal reason — scheduled, unscheduled, convenience, or robbed for another aircraft
  • Manufacturer and CAGE code, with NSN for ex-military stock
  • Rotable, repairable, expendable or consumable classification
  • Cost, vendor, purchase order and invoice
  • Warranty tracked by both calendar date and hours
  • Core returns and exchanges, with the deadline the core is due back
  • Quantity and unit of measure — each, feet, or quarts
  • Storage location and quarantine status
  • Attached documents and photos on any part or event

The fuel

The largest number you spend, logged like it matters.

Fuel is the biggest variable cost an airplane has, and the one most often recorded as a single dollar figure that answers nothing later. AeroTrax records the uplift, the units, the density and the whole invoice — so burn, economy and what a stop actually cost are figures that fall out rather than ones you reconstruct.

Every airplane

The uplift itself

Gallons, liters, pounds or kilograms — recorded in the unit it was actually sold in, with fuel on board before and after, and which tanks took it.

Density, not a guess

The specific gravity the FBO billed at, stored with the ticket. Jet A is nominally 6.7 lb/gal and rarely exactly that, and the gap is both money and a weight-and-balance error.

Full or partial, flagged

Whether a fill topped the tanks off. Every economy figure in the app rests on it, and it cannot be reconstructed later — so AeroTrax asks once and gets the burn right.

Burn per hour, computed

Gallons or pounds per hour derived between known-full fills, per leg and rolling. Not a number you maintain — one that falls out of the flights and fills you already logged.

Planned against actual

The variance, which is the figure that means something. An absolute burn tells you the airplane is normal; the drift from plan tells you something changed.

Price by airport, over time

What you paid where, trending. Enough to plan a route around fuel, or to notice the field twelve miles away has been ninety cents cheaper all year.

When it is a turbine

The whole invoice

Flowage, into-plane, ramp, handling, overnight, GPU and after-hours callout, each on its own line. The fuel price is rarely what the stop actually cost.

Contract fuel and releases

The release number, the contract price and the posted price side by side — so the savings your fuel program produced is a figure you can show rather than assume.

Waiver thresholds

The uplift minimum that waives the ramp fee, and whether you cleared it. Taking on 180 gallons and paying a $450 ramp fee is the sort of thing you fix once you can see it.

Tankering, answered

Whether carrying cheap fuel from the departure field actually paid once the burn penalty of carrying the weight is counted. The calculation every flight department does in a spreadsheet.

SAF blend and certificates

Blend percentage, batch reference and the certificate behind it — the paper trail that emissions reporting and book-and-claim both ask for.

Trips, cost centers and billing

Which leg, which trip, which cost center, and whether the leg was flown under Part 91 or Part 135 — because that decides the tax treatment and whether the fuel is billable to someone else.

Fuel weight comes from the density actually recorded, not a table constant — so the number reaching weight and balance is the number you were billed for. On a jet taking on eight thousand pounds, the difference between assumed and actual density is not a rounding error.

Also on every fuel record

  • Airport and FBO kept apart, because three FBOs on one field rarely agree on price
  • Supplier and brand, tracked separately from whoever pumped it
  • Ticket and invoice number, truck number and line technician
  • Service type — self-serve, full-serve, truck or single-point pressure refueling
  • Taxes broken out — federal excise, state, local and sales
  • Currency and exchange rate, with VAT flagged where it is reclaimable
  • Payment method — fuel card, credit card or direct bill
  • Additives — FSII concentration, biocide and static dissipator
  • Sump and water check performed, with the result
  • Photo of the fuel ticket attached to the record

What AeroTrax is not

AeroTrax is a personal record-keeping tool. It does not replace the maintenance records your aircraft is required to carry, the judgment or signoff of a certificated mechanic, or any entry a regulation requires you or your mechanic to make. Keep the records you are required to keep — use AeroTrax to know what is coming, and to have it all in one place when someone asks.
